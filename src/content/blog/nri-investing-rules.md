For Non-Resident Indians, investing back home involves a distinct set of accounts, rules, and tax considerations that differ meaningfully from resident investing — and getting the structure right at the outset avoids complications later.

The foundation is choosing the correct bank account structure. An NRE (Non-Resident External) account holds foreign earnings converted to rupees, and both principal and interest are fully repatriable and tax-free in India. An NRO (Non-Resident Ordinary) account is meant for income earned within India — rent, dividends, or other domestic income — and is subject to Indian tax, with repatriation subject to specific limits and documentation. An FCNR (Foreign Currency Non-Resident) account holds deposits in foreign currency itself, avoiding currency conversion risk on the deposit, though typically used for term deposits rather than active investing.

Mutual fund investment is generally straightforward for most NRIs, with funds accepting investments through NRE or NRO accounts, subject to KYC compliance specific to NRI status. It's worth noting that NRIs based in the US and Canada face additional restrictions with many fund houses, due to compliance requirements under FATCA — not all schemes are open to investors from these specific countries, so checking fund-specific eligibility before investing is essential.

Taxation for NRIs on Indian investments follows largely similar structural rules to residents — equity and debt fund capital gains taxation follows the same LTCG/STCG framework — but with an important difference: TDS (Tax Deducted at Source) applies at the time of redemption for NRI investors, at rates that can be higher than what a resident investor would face on the same gain, deducted upfront by the fund house rather than settled entirely at tax filing.

Double Taxation Avoidance Agreements (DTAA), which India has with many countries, can allow NRIs to claim credit in their country of residence for tax already paid in India, avoiding being taxed twice on the same income — but this requires proper documentation and is worth discussing with a cross-border tax consultant familiar with both jurisdictions.

Repatriation of investment proceeds — moving money back to the country of residence — is generally permitted for NRE-linked investments without significant restriction, while NRO-linked repatriation is subject to an annual limit and requires specific certification from a chartered accountant confirming taxes have been properly paid.

For NRIs maintaining financial ties to India, working with an expert experienced specifically in NRI regulations — not just general investment advice — makes a meaningful difference, given how easily account structure or documentation errors can create complications at the time of redemption or repatriation.
