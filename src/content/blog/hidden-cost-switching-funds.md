Chasing performance is one of the most natural instincts in investing — and one of the most consistently damaging. An investor sees a fund topping last year's charts, moves their money in, watches it underperform this year, exits for the new leader, and repeats the cycle. Each individual decision feels rational. The cumulative pattern is quietly destructive.

The core problem is that yesterday's top performer rarely stays on top. Market leadership rotates — the sector or style that outperformed last year is often not the one that outperformed next year, sometimes for structural reasons entirely unrelated to fund quality. An investor chasing last year's winner is, statistically, often buying near a performance peak and selling near a trough — the exact opposite of sound investing behaviour, executed with complete conviction each time.

Beyond the timing problem, frequent switching carries direct, quantifiable costs that are easy to underestimate. Exit loads on early redemptions, however small individually, add up across multiple switches. Each redemption within the short-term window can also trigger capital gains tax at a less favourable rate than long-term holdings would incur. None of these costs show up as a dramatic single loss — they're small, distributed frictions that quietly erode returns switch after switch.

There's also an opportunity cost that's harder to see but often larger than the direct fees: time out of the market during the transition, and — more significantly — resetting your holding period each time you switch, repeatedly starting the compounding clock over rather than letting it run uninterrupted. An investor who stayed in a merely decent, consistent fund for 15 years frequently outperforms one who chased five different "best" funds over the same period, purely because uninterrupted compounding beats repeatedly optimized entry points.

This doesn't mean funds should never be changed. There are legitimate reasons to switch — a genuine, sustained change in the fund's strategy or management, consistent multi-year underperformance against its own category (not just a single bad year), or a change in your own goals or risk profile. The distinction is between switching for a considered structural reason versus switching reactively because a chart looked different last quarter.

A useful discipline: before switching any fund, ask whether the reason would still hold up if you looked at 3-5 years of data rather than the most recent one. If the answer changes depending on the time window you choose, that's usually a signal to hold steady rather than react.

The investors who build the most wealth aren't the ones who found the perfect fund every year. They're the ones who found a reasonably good fund and had the discipline to stay.
