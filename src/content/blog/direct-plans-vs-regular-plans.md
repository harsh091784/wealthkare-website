A common piece of internet advice tells investors to always choose Direct Plans over Regular Plans because of the lower expense ratio. The logic sounds airtight — lower fees, higher returns, obviously better. But this advice misses what a Regular Plan actually provides, and for many investors, that omission matters more than the fee difference.

Direct Plans and Regular Plans hold the exact same underlying portfolio, managed by the same fund manager, with identical investment strategy. The only structural difference is the expense ratio — Regular Plans carry a slightly higher fee because that fee compensates the distributor for advice, service, and support. Direct Plans skip the distributor and the fee, but also skip everything the distributor provides.

Here's what gets lost in the "always choose Direct" narrative: most investors don't actually make purely rational, disciplined decisions on their own. They chase last year's top performer. They panic-sell during downturns. They neglect to rebalance. They pick funds based on advertisements rather than suitability. The behavioural cost of these mistakes routinely exceeds the fee savings from a Direct Plan by a wide margin — a single panicked exit at the wrong time can cost far more than years of expense ratio difference.

A Regular Plan, through a qualified distributor, provides structured guidance: help selecting funds appropriate to your goals and risk profile, portfolio reviews to catch drift before it becomes a problem, and — critically — a second voice during volatile markets, when the instinct to panic-sell is strongest. For someone with the time, discipline, and expertise to manage this themselves, Direct Plans make complete sense. For someone who doesn't, the extra fee often buys real value.

This isn't a case against Direct Plans for everyone — sophisticated, hands-on investors with the discipline to stay the course through volatility may genuinely be better served by them. It's a case against treating the fee difference as the only variable that matters, when behavioural discipline and structured guidance are just as real a factor in your final outcome.

The right question isn't "which plan has the lower expense ratio." It's "which arrangement will actually get me to invest consistently, appropriately, and without emotional missteps, for the next 15-20 years." For many investors, that answer isn't the one with the smallest number on the factsheet.
