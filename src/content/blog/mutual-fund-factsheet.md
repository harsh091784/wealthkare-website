Every mutual fund publishes a monthly factsheet — a two-page document packed with numbers that most investors glance at and quietly ignore. Yet learning to read it is one of the most useful skills an investor can develop. It's the difference between choosing a fund on reputation and understanding what you actually own.

Start with the basics at the top: the fund's category, its benchmark, and its assets under management. The category tells you the fund's mandate — large cap, mid cap, flexi cap, debt, and so on — which shapes both its risk and its role in your portfolio. The benchmark is the index the fund measures itself against; a fund that consistently trails its own benchmark is worth questioning.

Next, look at returns — but read them correctly. Factsheets show returns across multiple periods: one year, three years, five years, and since inception. Resist the temptation to fixate on the recent numbers. A fund that soared last year may simply have ridden a favourable trend. What you want is consistency across periods, especially the longer ones, ideally compared against both the benchmark and the category average.

Then examine the portfolio itself. The top holdings reveal where your money actually goes — the specific companies or instruments the fund owns. The sector allocation shows how concentrated or diversified the fund is. A fund heavily tilted toward one or two sectors carries more risk than a broadly spread one, even if recent returns look similar.

Pay close attention to two often-ignored numbers. The expense ratio is the annual fee you pay, deducted from your returns — and over decades, even small differences compound into meaningful sums. The portfolio turnover ratio indicates how frequently the fund manager buys and sells; very high turnover can signal an erratic strategy and higher costs.

For debt funds, a few additional metrics matter. Average maturity and duration tell you how sensitive the fund is to interest rate changes. Credit quality reveals how much risk the fund takes to earn its returns — a fund reaching for extra yield through lower-rated instruments is taking on risk that may not be obvious from returns alone.

Finally, look at the fund manager's tenure. A strategy is only as reliable as the person executing it, and a recent change in management means past performance was achieved by someone who may no longer be there.

None of this requires financial expertise — just a little patience and knowing where to look. The factsheet exists to inform you. Reading it turns you from a passive investor hoping for the best into an informed one who understands exactly what they hold and why.

Spend ten minutes with the factsheet before you invest. It's the cheapest due diligence you'll ever do.
