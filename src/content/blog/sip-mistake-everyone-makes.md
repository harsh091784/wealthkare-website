SIPs are often described as the safest, most disciplined way to invest — and largely, they are. But there's one mistake so common it deserves its own conversation: stopping the SIP exactly when it matters most.

Here's the pattern. Markets fall. The investor watches their portfolio value drop for a few months. Anxiety builds. Eventually, they pause or stop their SIP — reasoning that they'll "restart when things look better." It feels sensible in the moment. It is, almost without exception, the worst possible decision.

The entire mathematical advantage of a SIP comes from rupee-cost averaging — buying more units when prices are low and fewer when prices are high. A falling market isn't a signal to stop; it's the exact moment your SIP is doing its most valuable work, accumulating units at a discount. Stopping during a downturn means you miss the cheap units entirely, and only resume buying once prices have recovered — the worst possible sequence.

Consider two investors who both start a ₹10,000 SIP. Investor A continues through a market fall and recovery. Investor B pauses for six months during the dip, then resumes once markets have visibly recovered. Even though both eventually invest similar total amounts, Investor A ends up with meaningfully more units and a larger corpus — purely because they kept buying while prices were depressed.

Why does the mistake happen so consistently? It's psychological, not mathematical. Watching account values fall triggers loss aversion, a deeply wired human response. The market doesn't care about your emotions, but your decisions do — and emotional decisions during volatility are reliably the ones investors regret most in hindsight.

The practical defense is structural, not willpower-based. Automate your SIP so it doesn't require a monthly decision. Avoid checking your portfolio value obsessively during volatile periods — daily tracking amplifies anxiety without adding useful information. And if you genuinely need to reduce exposure, do it through a considered reallocation, not a panicked pause.

A SIP is a long-term commitment precisely because markets are unpredictable in the short term. The investors who benefit most aren't the ones who time it perfectly — they're the ones who simply never stopped.
