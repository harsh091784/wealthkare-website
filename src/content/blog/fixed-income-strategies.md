When equity markets turn volatile, the instinct for many investors is to exit entirely and sit in cash. A more measured response is strengthening the fixed income portion of a portfolio — the part designed specifically to hold steady when equity doesn't.

Fixed income isn't a single, uniform category. It spans a spectrum from the very safe to the moderately risky, and understanding where different instruments sit on that spectrum is essential during volatile periods, when the temptation is to treat "fixed income" as a single safe bucket.

Government securities and high-quality corporate bonds sit at the safer end — backed by sovereign or strong corporate credit, offering predictable income with low default risk. Debt mutual funds holding these instruments offer similar safety with added liquidity, though their NAV does fluctuate modestly with interest rate movements — a dynamic worth understanding rather than being surprised by.

Corporate fixed deposits, offered by NBFCs and companies, sit further along the risk spectrum — typically offering higher rates than bank FDs specifically because they carry more credit risk. During volatile periods, this is exactly where quality matters most: sticking to well-rated issuers (CRISIL or ICRA rated AA and above) rather than chasing the highest advertised rate, which often signals higher underlying risk.

Duration management becomes particularly relevant during periods of interest rate uncertainty. Longer-duration debt funds are more sensitive to interest rate changes — their NAV moves more sharply, in either direction, when rates shift. Shorter-duration funds and instruments offer more stability, at the cost of somewhat lower yield potential. During genuinely uncertain rate environments, favouring shorter duration reduces the risk of an unwelcome NAV swing.

A layered approach often works best: a liquid or ultra-short fund for near-term needs and emergency reserves, a short-to-medium duration quality debt fund for the stable core of a fixed income allocation, and selectively, a modest allocation to higher-yield instruments like corporate FDs for investors specifically seeking extra income and comfortable with the associated credit risk.

It's worth remembering what fixed income is meant to do in a portfolio during volatile times — provide ballast, not returns competitive with equity. Its job is to reduce overall portfolio volatility and provide a stable source of funds that doesn't require selling equity at a loss during a downturn. Judging fixed income by equity-like return expectations misunderstands its actual role.

Volatile markets test portfolio construction more than fund selection. A thoughtfully built fixed income allocation — matched to your actual liquidity needs and risk tolerance — is what allows an investor to stay calm and stay invested through the equity portion of their portfolio riding out its own turbulence.
