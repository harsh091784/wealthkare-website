If you're salaried, the tax regime decision is more predictable than it feels. Your income structure gives you clearer signals than most people realize — you just need to know where to look.

The single biggest factor for salaried employees is HRA. If you live in rented accommodation in a metro city and claim substantial HRA exemption, the Old Regime often wins decisively — HRA alone can shelter a large chunk of income that the New Regime simply cannot touch. If you live in your own home or don't claim HRA, this advantage disappears entirely.

The second factor is how disciplined you already are about tax-saving investments. If you're maxing out 80C through EPF, PPF, or ELSS, adding 80D for health insurance, and contributing to NPS under 80CCD(1B), your combined deductions can easily cross ₹3-4 lakhs. At that level, the Old Regime's higher slabs are usually more than offset by the lower taxable base.

But here's the shift worth noting: the New Regime's standard deduction has increased in recent budgets, and its slabs have become genuinely competitive — especially for incomes under ₹15 lakhs with minimal other deductions. A young professional early in their career, living with parents, not yet claiming HRA, and investing directly in mutual funds rather than tax-saving instruments, will very often find the New Regime cheaper.

There's a middle-income trap worth watching. Between ₹10-15 lakhs, the gap between regimes narrows considerably, and small changes in your deduction profile can flip which one wins. This is exactly the income band where running the actual numbers matters most — general advice becomes unreliable precisely where precision matters.

One practical note: salaried employees can switch regimes every financial year (unlike those with business income, who face restrictions). This means your choice isn't permanent — revisit it annually, especially after a salary hike, a new home loan, or a change in your investment pattern.

The honest answer to "which regime should I pick" is: run both calculations with this year's actual numbers, not last year's assumption.
