Retirement planning is the most important, highest-stakes financial calculation most people never actually do. It's easy to postpone precisely because the deadline feels distant — until it isn't, and the math no longer offers the flexibility it once did.

The starting point isn't a savings target; it's understanding what your future expenses will actually look like. Your current monthly expenses need to be projected forward to your retirement date, accounting for inflation — and inflation over a 20-30 year working career compounds into a dramatically larger number than intuition suggests. ₹75,000 in monthly expenses today becomes over ₹4 lakhs monthly in 30 years at 6% inflation — a number that surprises most people the first time they calculate it.

From that inflated expense figure, the next step is estimating the corpus needed to sustain it through retirement itself — commonly 20-30 years, given rising life expectancy. This isn't simply expenses multiplied by retirement years; the corpus continues earning returns during retirement too, which extends how long it can sustain a given withdrawal rate, but must also account for continued inflation during retirement itself, since expenses keep rising even after you stop earning.

Once the target corpus is clear, the accumulation strategy should follow an age-appropriate glide path. In your 20s and 30s, decades from retirement, a heavily equity-oriented portfolio makes sense — you have time to ride out volatility for higher long-term growth. As retirement approaches, inside the final 5-10 years, the allocation should progressively shift toward debt and more stable instruments, protecting the accumulated corpus from a poorly timed market downturn right before you need to start withdrawing.

A well-structured retirement plan typically draws on multiple instruments working together: EPF and NPS providing a disciplined, tax-efficient core; PPF adding a guaranteed, risk-free component; equity mutual funds through SIPs providing the growth engine during the accumulation years; and increasingly, as retirement nears, fixed income and annuity products providing stability and predictable income.

One frequently underestimated factor is healthcare. Medical inflation in India runs well above general inflation, and healthcare costs in retirement — precisely when income has stopped — can be the single largest unplanned drain on a retirement corpus if not accounted for separately, through adequate health insurance that continues into retirement, not just during working years.

The single most powerful lever in retirement planning is time — starting even a decade earlier dramatically reduces the required monthly contribution, due to the extended compounding runway. The second most powerful lever is consistency — reviewing and adjusting the plan periodically, rather than setting a number once in your 20s and assuming it remains accurate three decades later.

Retirement planning rewards those who start early and revisit often, far more than those who wait for an ideal moment to begin seriously calculating.
