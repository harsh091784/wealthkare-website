Every financial advisor says the same thing: start early. It's repeated so often it's become background noise — a cliché people nod along to without absorbing. But the actual magnitude of what delay costs is rarely made concrete, and once you see the numbers, the advice stops sounding like a platitude.

Consider two investors. The first starts a ₹10,000 monthly SIP at age 25 and continues for 35 years until 60, investing a total of ₹42 lakhs. The second waits until 35 to start the same ₹10,000 SIP, investing for 25 years until 60 — a total of ₹30 lakhs. The second investor actually invested only ₹12 lakhs less in total contributions. Yet at a steady growth assumption, the first investor's final corpus can end up nearly double the second's — not because they invested dramatically more, but because their money had ten additional years to compound.

This is the part delay genuinely costs you: not the missed contributions, but the missed compounding on those contributions, and — just as importantly — the missed compounding on the growth those contributions would have generated in their own early years. The earliest money invested does the most work, because it has the longest runway.

This is why the specific years lost to delay matter more than the years gained later. Someone who starts at 25 and stops at 30, then never invests again, can still end up with a larger corpus at 60 than someone who starts at 35 and invests diligently every year until retirement — purely because of when the money entered the market, not how much or for how long in total.

The psychological trap is that delay never feels costly in the moment. Skipping this year to start "next year, once things settle" feels harmless — a rounding error. It's only years later, doing the comparison, that the true cost becomes visible, and by then it's unrecoverable. You cannot buy back lost compounding years at any price.

The practical implication isn't about finding a large sum to invest. It's about starting with whatever amount is available today, rather than waiting for an ideal amount, an ideal market, or an ideal moment that rarely, if ever, actually arrives. A modest SIP started now consistently outperforms a larger SIP started later.

The best time to start was years ago. The second-best time is today.
