Gold occupies a unique place in Indian household finance — part investment, part cultural tradition, part emotional security. This dual identity often leads to two extremes: households that hold far more gold than makes financial sense, purely out of habit, or modern investors who dismiss it entirely as an outdated asset class. Both miss what gold actually does well in a portfolio.

Gold's genuine financial role is as a diversifier and a hedge — not as a primary growth engine. Historically, gold has tended to perform well during periods of high inflation, currency weakness, or global economic uncertainty — conditions where equity and even debt can struggle simultaneously. This low or negative correlation with other asset classes is gold's real value: it doesn't necessarily move with your other investments, which means it can cushion a portfolio precisely when other assets are under pressure.

What gold is not particularly good at is generating long-term growth. Over multi-decade periods, equity has generally significantly outpaced gold's returns. An investor treating gold as their primary wealth-building asset, rather than a stabilizing allocation, is very likely under-compounding their overall wealth compared to a more equity-oriented approach.

This is why most sound allocation guidelines suggest gold as a modest portion of an overall portfolio — commonly in the range of 5-15%, depending on individual risk profile and goals — rather than either a token 1-2% or a dominant 40-50% position. At the modest end of that range, gold provides real diversification benefit without meaningfully dragging down long-term growth.

The form of gold exposure matters too. Physical gold — jewellery, coins, bars — carries making charges, storage risk, and purity concerns, and jewellery specifically loses significant value the moment it's purchased, given the labour cost embedded in the price that isn't recovered on resale. Gold ETFs and Sovereign Gold Bonds offer more efficient, cost-effective exposure to gold's price movement without these frictions — SGBs additionally offer a small annual interest and tax benefits on redemption at maturity that physical gold cannot match.

For many Indian families, some gold holding is also a cultural and emotional reality that exists alongside, not purely within, an investment framework — jewellery for a wedding, for instance, serves a purpose no allocation model captures. That's a legitimate consideration, separate from the purely financial one. The useful distinction is treating gold held for cultural or emotional reasons as separate from gold held as a financial allocation, rather than conflating the two when assessing whether your portfolio is appropriately diversified.

Gold has a real, useful role in a well-constructed portfolio — as a stabilizer, not a centerpiece. Understanding that distinction helps size the allocation sensibly, rather than by tradition or trend alone.
