It's the conversation nobody wants to have. Estate planning — deciding what happens to your wealth after you're gone — sits permanently at the bottom of most families' to-do lists. Not because it's unimportant, but because it forces us to confront something uncomfortable. Yet avoiding it doesn't make the need disappear; it only leaves your family to face the consequences unprepared.

In India, the reluctance runs especially deep. There's a cultural discomfort with discussing death and inheritance, a belief that these matters will somehow sort themselves out, and often a simple assumption that "my family knows what I want." But wishes that live only in your head have no legal standing. When a person passes away without a valid will, their assets are distributed according to succession law — which may bear little resemblance to what they actually intended.

The costs of this avoidance are real and often painful. Families are forced into lengthy legal processes to establish their claim to assets. Bank accounts get frozen, property titles get tangled, and investments become inaccessible for months or years. Worse, the absence of clear instructions frequently breeds disputes among people who otherwise loved each other — turning grief into conflict at the worst possible time.

Estate planning is simpler than most people fear. At its core, it involves a few key steps. First, a will — a clear, legally valid document stating who receives what. Second, nominations across all your accounts, insurance policies, and investments, kept up to date. Third, for larger or more complex estates, structures like trusts that can provide control, tax efficiency, and protection.

Nomination deserves special attention, because it's widely misunderstood. A nominee is not automatically the owner of an asset — they are, in law, a custodian who receives the asset on behalf of the legal heirs. This is why a will matters even when nominations exist: the will determines rightful ownership, while the nomination simply eases the transfer. Getting both aligned prevents a great deal of future confusion.

The right time to begin is now — not when you're older, not when you're wealthier, not "someday." Estate planning isn't only for the elderly or the ultra-rich. Anyone with dependents, property, or savings has an estate worth directing thoughtfully. A young parent with a single home and a term insurance policy needs a plan just as much as a business owner with sprawling assets.

The kindest thing you can do for the people you love is to make their hardest day a little easier. A clear plan, drawn up while you're healthy and thinking clearly, is a final act of care — sparing your family confusion, conflict, and cost when they're least able to bear it.

Start the conversation. It's uncomfortable for an afternoon, and invaluable for a lifetime.
