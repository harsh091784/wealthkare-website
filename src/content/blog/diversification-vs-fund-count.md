A common misconception equates diversification with quantity — the more funds you own, the more diversified you must be. In practice, many investors who hold 12, 15, even 20 mutual funds are barely more diversified than someone holding 3 well-chosen ones. Often, they're simply holding overlapping versions of the same exposure, multiplied.

True diversification means spreading risk across genuinely different sources of return — different asset classes, different market caps, different sectors, different investment styles. Owning five large-cap equity funds from five different fund houses isn't diversification in any meaningful sense; those funds likely hold many of the same blue-chip stocks, move together in most market conditions, and offer little protection from each other during a downturn.

This pattern — sometimes called "diworsification" — tends to happen gradually. An investor starts a SIP, reads about another promising fund, adds it, then another, and another, each addition feeling like a reasonable diversification step. Years later, they're managing a sprawling, overlapping portfolio that's genuinely harder to track, harder to rebalance, and not meaningfully less risky than a much simpler one would have been.

Real diversification is better achieved through structure than volume. A portfolio spread across large cap, mid cap, and a debt fund — three funds — genuinely diversifies across market capitalization and asset class. Adding a tenth large-cap fund to that mix adds complexity without adding meaningful protection.

There's also a cost to over-diversification beyond the illusion of safety. Tracking, reviewing, and rebalancing 15 funds consumes far more time and attention than 4-5 well-chosen ones — and in practice, most investors with sprawling portfolios review them less rigorously than those with focused ones, simply because the task feels overwhelming. Fewer funds, thoughtfully chosen and genuinely monitored, frequently outperform a large, neglected collection.

A practical test: for each fund in your portfolio, ask what specific role it plays that another fund doesn't already cover. If the honest answer is "not much, it just seemed like a good fund at the time," that's a candidate for consolidation.

Diversification is a tool for managing risk intelligently — not a number of funds to accumulate. A focused, well-structured portfolio of a handful of genuinely different funds typically serves an investor far better than a large, overlapping collection that looks diversified on paper but isn't in practice.
